Braking Points: Find Yours — Then Move It

By Lena Voss, RaceHero's AI coach · Part 1 — Braking · Chapter 1

The short answer

A braking point is a fixed reference beside the track — a marker board, a painted line, the start of a kerb — that tells you where to begin braking for a corner. It is not a feeling, and it is not a guess. Because the reference never moves, it turns your braking into something measurable: start there, lap after lap, then move it deeper a car length at a time as your laps prove they can take it. If you can't name the thing you braked at, you don't have a braking point yet. This chapter fixes that.

Read what she said

Here's the truth about braking points: most drivers don't have one. They have a feeling — somewhere around here feels right — and feelings move. They move with traffic, with tired tires, with one bad lap. A braking point is a reference: something you can see at speed. A board. A line across the track. The edge of a shadow. Pick one you cannot miss. Then start early — deliberately early — and walk it deeper, a car length at a time, only while the laps stay clean. And remember, the point where you start braking is only half of it. Where you finish — how you come off the pedal — decides the whole corner. So the next time you brake for a corner, ask yourself one question: what did I just brake at? If you can't name it, you don't have a braking point yet. You have a habit. Let's fix the habit.

The lesson, in her words

Three ideas carry this whole chapter. Everything after this card is the deep read.

The reference

A braking point is something you can see at speed — a board, a line, a change in the surface. Not a distance in your head, not a feeling in your stomach. Feelings move with traffic, tires and nerves. The board doesn't.

The creep

Nobody finds their limit by jumping at it. Start deliberately early, hold the point until the laps are boringly clean, then walk it deeper — a car length at a time. Later is earned in small steps, never guessed in one big one.

The finish

The point where you start braking is only half the skill. Where you finish — how the pedal comes off, and the speed you're carrying at turn-in — decides whether the corner works. A perfect start with a clumsy release is still a slow corner.

Name your marker. Move it on evidence. Finish the stop like you mean it.

The deep read

How to find your braking point

What a braking point actually is

Strip it down and a braking point is a contract between your eyes and your right foot. Your eyes find a fixed object; when it arrives, your foot moves. No judgement call at 180 km/h, no negotiation. That is the entire mechanism — and it's why the reference has to live outside your head. Anything internal — a count, a feel for distance, a sense of "about here" — degrades exactly when you need it most: in traffic, on worn tires, on the lap after a mistake.

It's also bigger than a single point. A complete braking reference has three parts: where you start (the point itself), how hard you hit the pedal at the top, and where you finish — the speed you're carrying as you turn in. This chapter is mostly about the first. The finish gets its own chapters later in Part 1, because it's where most of the lap time actually hides.

Choosing a reference you can see at speed

Good references share three traits: they're fixed, they're visible at full speed, and they're unmistakable — there is exactly one of them in your field of view at the moment it matters. In rough order of reliability:

  • Marker boards — the numbered boards beside most braking zones exist for precisely this. The catch: on a busy day, "the 100 board" is where everyone brakes. Your point is yours; the board is just the ruler you measure it from ("half a board past the 100").
  • Painted lines and surface changes — a line across the track, the seam where old tarmac meets new, the start of a kerb. Surface changes are excellent: you can often feel them through the car as well as see them, which gives you two senses confirming the same point.
  • Shadows, posts and track furniture — usable, with a warning: shadows move with the sun and vanish under cloud. Fine as a backup, dangerous as your only reference.
  • Another car — never. The car ahead brakes where it brakes, earlier for its own reasons, and following its point into the corner is how trains of drivers all end up slower than any of them alone.

One more rule: pick the reference with your eyes where they'll actually be. At the end of a straight your eyes should already be travelling toward the turn-in — so the best braking references sit in your peripheral vision as you look through the corner, not dead ahead where you stopped looking two seconds ago.

The creep: how braking points get later

Here is the method, and it is deliberately unglamorous. On your first laps at a corner, brake early on purpose — early enough that the entry is almost boring. Choose your reference and brake at it every lap until you can do it without thinking. Only then, move the point deeper: one car length. Hold it there until those laps are clean too. Repeat.

Two things make this work. First, the steps are small enough that a mistake is a wide entry, not a crash — you're exploring the limit, not leaping past it. Second, every step is proven before it's kept. A braking point you reached on one heroic lap and can't repeat isn't yours; it's a story. The point you own is the deepest one you can hit ten laps in a row.

And when a step goes wrong — you arrive too hot, the entry unravels — that's information, not failure. Come back one step, hold it, and let the deeper point wait for another session, fresher tires, or a better exit onto the straight. Braking points are set as much by the speed you arrive with as by the corner itself.

The start is half of it: finishing the stop

Watch a fast driver's brake trace and the start isn't even the interesting part. The hit matters — a firm, decisive initial application, up near the tires' limit, because a tentative squeeze that slowly builds wastes metres you paid for with speed. But what separates drivers is the release.

As the car slows, it needs less brake to keep decelerating — a car with downforce literally loses grip as speed bleeds off, and even in a kart the tires' capacity changes as load comes off the front. Hold one static pressure all the way down and you'll either lock a front late in the stop or arrive at turn-in still leaning on the pedal. So the pressure has to decay as the speed decays, smoothly, in one motion — hard at the top, easing through the middle, and trailing away to nothing at the apex. Reading it in your data

You don't need telemetry to use this chapter — the reference method works with your eyes alone. But if you have a trace, the braking point stops being a memory and becomes a fact. Three things to look at: where the brake trace rises against track position (is it the same place every lap?), how it rises (one decisive hit, or a squeeze that takes a second to arrive?), and where it ends relative to turn-in. The classic amateur signature is a plot shaped like a plus sign: brake fully, release fully, coast, then turn — every input separated, none of them overlapping. Fast traces blend. They rise once, decay once, and hand over to steering and throttle without a dead zone in between.

This is also where honesty helps: if your brake point wanders by ten metres lap to lap, no amount of bravery will fix your corner. Fix the wandering first. Consistency is not the boring prerequisite of speed — on the brakes, it is speed, because only a repeatable point can be moved.

Common mistakes

  • Braking by feel. "About here" is not a point. Session by session it drifts earlier as you tire and later as you get confident, and neither drift is a decision you made.
  • Chasing "later" by braking harder. A later point comes from a moved reference, not a bigger hit. Braking harder at the same point just arrives at the same place more violently, with less left for the finish.
  • Moving two things at once. If you change the braking point and the turn-in in the same session, you'll never know which one worked. One variable per run of laps.
  • Keeping the heroic lap. The deepest point you survived once is not your braking point. Your braking point is the deepest one you can repeat — build from there.
  • Borrowing the car ahead's point. Their point serves their car, their tires and their exit. Follow it and you've outsourced the most important decision on the lap.

The drill: one corner, one marker

Take this to your next session. It needs one corner and about fifteen minutes.

1

Pick and name it

Choose one corner. Find your reference and name it out loud — "the seam before the 100 board." Brake there, deliberately early, for three laps.

2

Creep on clean laps

Move the point one car length deeper. Hold it until you get three clean laps in a row. Messy lap? The step doesn't count — repeat it, don't advance.

3

Stop at the first wobble

When a step makes the entry untidy twice, come back one step and finish the session there. That point — repeatable, owned — is today's braking point. Write it down.

Next session, you start from the written point — not from a memory of roughly where it was. That's the whole system: a reference, small steps, evidence. It works at a kart track, on a track day, and in the sim, because it was never about the car. It's about giving your right foot an order it can actually follow.

Questions

Asked at the pit wall

The three questions this chapter always gets.

What is a braking point in racing?

A fixed visual reference beside the track — a marker board, a line, a surface change — that tells you where to start braking for a corner. Because it never moves, it turns braking from a feeling into a measurement you can repeat and adjust, a car length at a time.

How do I find my braking point for a corner?

Pick a reference you can see at full speed, start braking deliberately early, and move the point deeper in small steps — about a car length at a time — only while your laps stay clean. The moment a lap gets untidy, the point was too deep for today; come back one step and hold it there.

How do I brake later without crashing?

You don't brake later by braking harder — you brake later by moving a known reference. Hold one marker, build consistency at it first, then creep it deeper in small steps while keeping the finish of the stop clean. If you can't name the reference you braked at, you're guessing — and guessing later is what ends in the gravel.
